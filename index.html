Kerbside waste across Victoria's 79 councils

Where Victoria's rubbish goes

The average Victorian household puts out about 16 kilograms a week — a full wheelie bin, every week, from every house in the state. A little over half of it never reaches landfill.

Which half, and why, turns out to have almost nothing to do with the thing everyone assumes.

Four bins, and one number that matters

Victoria is moving every council onto a four-bin system, and not every council is there yet. Throughout this page, diversion means the share of kerbside material kept out of landfill. It is the single number councils are judged on.

Garbage — the red-lid bin

Everything that goes to landfill. Collected weekly almost everywhere.

1,108,000 tonnes a year

Food and garden — the lime lid

Garden clippings, and in most councils food scraps too. Known as FOGO, short for food organics and garden organics.

718,000 tonnes a year

Mixed recycling — the yellow lid

Paper, plastics and metals, sorted at a materials recovery facility, or MRF.

503,000 tonnes a year

Glass — the purple lid

Glass kept separate so it does not shatter and contaminate the yellow bin. Only 16 councils had one in 2023–24.

16,000 tonnes a year

Colours on this page are a colour-blind-safe set, not the real lid colours, so that every chart stays readable. The same colour means the same stream everywhere.

Why

Councils publish their waste figures, but one council at a time and in spreadsheets nobody reads. Side by side they show a gap in service that residents have no easy way to see.

Who

Any Victorian who puts a bin out. No statistical background assumed, and every term defined where it first appears.

What

Five government datasets: 23 years of kerbside tonnages and five years of service costs for all 79 councils, plus council boundaries, 664 waste facilities and the 2021 census socio-economic indexes. All listed at the foot of the page.

How

Maps carry anything geographic, because much of the pattern here is about where you live. Distributions use one dot per council rather than a smoothed curve, so you can count the councils behind every claim.

01 — How much

The green bin now outweighs the recycling bin

Most people picture recycling as the yellow bin. By weight it is the smallest of the three that matter.

Nearly a fifth of the yellow bin is lost

18.7 per cent of what Victorians put in the recycling bin in 2023–24 never became anything, up from 5.3 per cent in 2015–16.

The widening starts around 2017–18. Several things changed in the recycling industry over those years, and this data can show the loss but cannot attribute it to any one of them.

So what actually explains it?

It is not the city–country divide

Metropolitan councils keep 50.2 per cent out of landfill. Regional councils keep 50.5. They spend $138 and $141 a bin. The comparison everyone reaches for first is a rounding error.

What separates a good council from a poor one is far more ordinary: which bins it gives you.

03 — The mechanism

A green bin is worth twenty-three points of diversion

Councils with a food and garden bin keep a median 52.6 per cent out of landfill. Councils without one manage 29.6. Nothing else in this data comes close to that effect.

Where councils fall, by the bins they offer

Every dot is one council. Dots stack where several councils land in the same three-point band, so each row's height shows how many councils are behind it. Dashed line marks the row median. 2024–25.

Local Government Victoria, LGPRF indicator WC5, and Recycling Victoria, VLGAS release 2025.03

Three councils bought the bin that does not help

Adding a green bin moves the median from 30.6 to 53.8 per cent. Adding a fourth bin for glass on top of that moves it to 52.4 — in other words, not at all. The gain is almost entirely in the third bin.

Which makes the third row worth a second look. Hindmarsh, Yarra and Yarriambiack run a separate glass bin but still have no green bin. Hindmarsh and Yarriambiack rank 77th and 78th of 79 for diversion.

Three councils is three councils, not a trend — the row is deliberately drawn so you can count them. But they are the only councils in Victoria to have added the bin worth nothing before the bin worth twenty-three points.

04 — Why some councils cannot

A green bin needs somewhere for the food to go

Food and garden waste has to be trucked to a composting plant. Where there is no plant within reach, the bin is expensive to run — which is the usual explanation for the councils that do not have one.

Victoria's 664 waste facilities are not evenly spread

One circle per council area, sized by how many waste and resource recovery facilities sit inside it, coloured by whether any of them processes organics. March 2025.

Recycling Victoria, waste and resource recovery infrastructure map data, March 2025. Seven of the 664 sites are recorded against alpine resorts or suburbs rather than councils and are not mapped.

Infrastructure follows population, and population follows the coast

Greater Dandenong alone holds 47 sites. Across the Wimmera and the Mallee there are council areas with none at all.

How far the food would have to travel

A line from each of the 13 councils with no green bin to the nearest organics processing plant. Diamonds mark the plants.

Recycling Victoria, VLGAS release 2025.03 and waste infrastructure map data, March 2025. Straight-line distance from the council's centre, not road distance. "Organics recycling" in the source covers all organics processing, not composting alone.

Distance explains twelve of the thirteen

Glenelg is 103 kilometres from the nearest plant, Hindmarsh 86, West Wimmera 80. The typical council that does run a green bin is 13 kilometres away.

Yarra is the exception. An inner-Melbourne council four kilometres from the CBD, it sits 12 kilometres from a plant — closer than the median council with a green bin. Distance is not what is stopping Yarra.

05 — What it costs

The councils that bury the most also pay the most

Recycling is usually assumed to be the expensive option. Across Victoria the relationship runs the other way.

What your council spends on each of your bins

One row per council, ordered by what a garbage bin costs. The hollow dot is the recycling bin, the solid dot the garbage bin, and the bar between them is coloured by how much the council keeps out of landfill. 2024–25.

Local Government Victoria, LGPRF indicators WC3 and WC5, 2024–25. Cost is the council's own reported direct cost per kerbside bin lift service, per year.

Two things read straight off the chart

The recycling bin costs about half the garbage bin — $72 against $138 for the median council, and cheaper in 75 of the 79. The four near the bottom with the hollow dot on the right are the exceptions.

And the colour darkens as you go down the list. Councils in the cheapest quarter keep a median 66 per cent out of landfill; councils in the dearest quarter keep 39.

The two move together, which is not the same as one causing the other. Small remote councils face high costs and long distances to a processing plant at the same time, and both are in this chart.

06 — Who gets what

Better-off councils divert more, but they are not the ones getting the bins

If performance tracks advantage, the obvious worry is that poorer areas are being left without services. The data supports the first half of that and not the second.

Socio-economic position against diversion

Two measures on one map. Across: the census index of disadvantage, where 1000 is the national average. Down: the share kept out of landfill. The darkest councils score high on both.

ABS SEIFA 2021 and Local Government Victoria, 2024–25. An association, not a cause — see the note below.

Missing out on a green bin is not about disadvantage

Every council is one tick. The upper row is the 66 councils with a green bin, the lower row the 13 without. Black ticks mark each row's median.

ABS SEIFA 2021 and Recycling Victoria, VLGAS release 2025.03

The 13 are scattered, not clustered

The two rows have almost the same median — 1003 for councils with a green bin, 991 for those without, against a national average of 1000 — and the 13 are spread across the whole range rather than bunched at the disadvantaged end.

So service access tracks council size and distance, not wealth. Worth stating plainly, because it is the opposite of what the map above might suggest.

A caution on that map. Metropolitan councils score highest on advantage but only middling on diversion, while regional cities score lower and divert most. The relationship is real but tangled up with council type.

Where that leaves it

Thirteen councils are one bin away

Victoria's recycling story is usually told as a contest between the city and the bush. The numbers do not support that. What they show instead is a service gap: thirteen councils, most of them small and remote, still without the green bin the rest of the state has — and keeping barely 30 per cent of their waste out of landfill as a result.

Twelve of them are a long way from anywhere that could process the food. Yarra is twelve kilometres from a plant. Three of them already found the money for a fourth bin, and spent it on glass.

The state has set 2030 as the deadline for four bins everywhere. On the rollout's current pace, the third bin is the one that would move these numbers most.

Notes on the data

  • Diversion figures are each council's own reported result under the performance reporting framework, not a figure derived here. Where a longer run of years was needed, diversion is computed from tonnages instead; the two agree closely.
  • "Never recovered" covers contamination and processing losses together, not contamination alone.
  • "No green bin" means 13 councils with no kerbside organics collection. "Only two bins" means the 10 of those that also have no glass bin. The other three run a glass bin instead.
  • Distances to processing plants are straight-line from the council's geographic centre, which understates the real road journey.
  • Victoria's container deposit scheme began in November 2023, so it falls only partly inside the latest year of data and is not treated as a measured effect.
  • Colours use the Okabe-Ito palette, which stays distinguishable under all three common forms of colour vision deficiency. They are not the real bin lid colours.

Use of generative AI

Generative AI (Claude) was used to locate and verify the data sources, to debug Vega-Lite specifications, to compute the Dorling cartogram circle positions, and to copy-edit the narrative text. All figures were computed directly from the source datasets listed above.
