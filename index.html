Kerbside waste across Victoria's 79 councils

Where Victoria's rubbish goes

Every household in Victoria puts out two, three or four bins. Together they add up to 2.35 million tonnes a year, and a little over half of it never reaches landfill. Which half depends on something most people never think about: how many bins their council hands out.

Four bins, and one number that matters

Victoria is moving every council onto a four-bin system. Not every council is there yet, and the gap between those that are and those that are not turns out to explain more about recycling performance than anything else on this page.

Red lid — garbage

Everything that goes to landfill. Collected weekly almost everywhere.

1,108,000 tonnes a year

Lime lid — food and garden

Garden clippings, and in most councils food scraps too. Known as FOGO, short for food organics and garden organics.

718,000 tonnes a year

Yellow lid — mixed recycling

Paper, plastics and metals, sorted at a materials recovery facility, or MRF.

503,000 tonnes a year

Purple lid — glass

Glass kept separate so it does not shatter and contaminate the yellow bin. Only 16 councils had one in 2023–24.

16,000 tonnes a year

Why

Councils publish their waste figures, but only one council at a time and in spreadsheets nobody reads. Put side by side, they show a gap in service that residents have no easy way to see.

Who

Any Victorian who puts a bin out. No statistical background assumed, and every term defined where it first appears.

What

Five government datasets: 23 years of kerbside tonnages and five years of service costs for all 79 councils, plus council boundaries, 664 waste facilities and the 2021 census socio-economic indexes. All listed at the foot of the page.

How

Diversion is the share of kerbside material kept out of landfill. It is the single number councils are judged on, and it anchors most of what follows. Maps carry anything geographic, because the pattern here is about where you live.

01 — How much

The green bin now outweighs the recycling bin

Most people picture recycling as the yellow bin. By weight it is the smallest of the three that matter. Food and garden waste overtook it years ago.

18.7% of what goes in the yellow bin never becomes anything, up from 5.3 per cent in 2015–16.

The jump starts in 2017–18, when China stopped accepting the world's imported recycling and Victoria's processing market fell apart.

So what actually explains it?

It is not the city–country divide

Metropolitan councils keep 50.2 per cent out of landfill. Regional councils keep 50.5. They spend $138 and $141 a bin. The comparison everyone reaches for first is a rounding error.

What separates a good council from a poor one is far more ordinary: how many bins it gives you.

03 — The mechanism

A green bin is worth twenty points of diversion

Councils with a food and garden bin keep about half their waste out of landfill. Councils without one manage barely a third. Nothing else in this data comes close to that effect.

Where councils fall, by how many bins they offer

Each curve shows how councils with that service are spread across the diversion range, 2024–25.

Recycling Victoria and Local Government Victoria

What to look at

The red curve is the ten councils still on two bins. Its peak sits around 31 per cent.

Add a green bin and the whole distribution shifts right, to a median of 52. Adding a fourth bin for glass barely moves it again — the gain is almost entirely in that third bin.

Thirteen councils still have no green bin

Councils without a kerbside food and garden collection, 2023–24.

Recycling Victoria, VLGAS 2025.03

The exception worth noticing

Twelve of the thirteen are rural shires in the state's west and east, where long distances to a processing facility make a green bin expensive to run. Yarra is the thirteenth — an inner-Melbourne council four kilometres from the CBD, where the obstacle is not distance but apartments with nowhere to put a third bin.

04 — What it costs

Councils that keep more out of landfill spend less, not more

Recycling is usually assumed to be the expensive option. Across Victoria's councils the relationship runs the other way, and reasonably strongly.

Cost against diversion, and how four councils moved

Grey dots are all 79 councils in 2024–25. Dashed lines mark the state medians. Coloured paths trace four councils across five years.

Local Government Victoria, LGPRF indicators WC3 and WC5

What to look at

Most councils sit in the green or the plain quadrants. Very few manage to spend a lot and still bury most of it.

Knox and Greater Bendigo moved up and to the left: better results, lower cost. Casey and Yarra went the other way.

A likely reason is the landfill levy. A tonne in the green bin avoids a charge that has been rising steeply, so diverting waste is now often the cheaper option.

05 — Who gets what

Better-off councils divert more, but they are not the ones getting the bins

If performance tracks advantage, the obvious worry is that poorer areas are being left without services. The data supports the first half of that and not the second.

Socio-economic position against diversion

Two measures on one map. Across: the ABS index of disadvantage, where 1000 is the national average. Down: the share kept out of landfill. The darkest blue-green councils score high on both.

ABS SEIFA 2021 and Local Government Victoria, 2024–25. An association, not a cause — see the note below.

Missing out on a green bin is not about disadvantage

How councils with and without a food and garden bin are spread across the socio-economic scale.

ABS SEIFA 2021 and Recycling Victoria, VLGAS 2025.03

What to look at

The two curves sit almost exactly on top of each other. Median 982 for councils with a green bin, 968 for those without.

So service access tracks council size and distance, not wealth. Worth stating plainly, because it is the opposite of what the first map might suggest.

A caution. Metropolitan councils score highest on advantage but only middling on diversion, while regional cities score lower and divert most. The relationship is real but tangled up with council type.

06 — What changed

Twenty years of slow repair, and one sharp shock

The green bin did not arrive all at once. It spread council by council over a decade, while the recycling system it sat alongside took the worst hit in its history.

Where that leaves it

Thirteen councils are one bin away

Victoria's recycling story is usually told as a contest between the city and the bush. The numbers do not support that. What they show instead is a service gap: thirteen councils, most of them small and remote, still running the two-bin system the rest of the state left behind — and diverting barely a third of their waste as a result.

The state has set 2030 as the deadline for four bins everywhere. On the rollout's current pace, that is the single change that would move these numbers most.

Notes on the data

  • Diversion figures are each council's own reported result under the performance reporting framework, not a figure derived here. Where a longer run of years was needed, diversion is computed from tonnages instead; the two agree closely.
  • "Collected but not recovered" covers contamination and processing losses together, not contamination alone.
  • Victoria's container deposit scheme began in November 2023, so it falls only partly inside the latest year of data and is not treated as a measured effect.
