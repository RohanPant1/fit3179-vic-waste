Kerbside waste in Victoria's 79 councils

Where Victoria's rubbish goes

Every week the average Victorian household puts out about 16 kg of rubbish, recycling and garden waste. A bit over half of it ends up somewhere other than landfill.

Some councils do a lot better than that and some do a lot worse. It's easy to assume the difference is city versus country. This page looks at whether that's true, and what actually explains it.

Before you start

Four bins and one number

The number this page keeps coming back to is the diversion rate: the share of a council's kerbside waste that gets recycled or composted instead of buried in landfill. If your council's rate is 50%, then for every 10 kg your household puts out, 5 kg gets used again and 5 kg goes into the ground. Higher is better.

Red lid: garbage

Everything that goes to landfill. Collected weekly almost everywhere.

1.11 Mt in 2023–24

Lime lid: food and garden

Garden clippings, and in 55 councils food scraps too (FOGO). Composted.

718 kt in 2023–24

Yellow lid: recycling

Paper, plastics and metals, sorted at a materials recovery facility.

503 kt in 2023–24

Purple lid: glass

Kept separate so it doesn't shatter through the yellow bin. 16 councils.

16 kt in 2023–24

These four colours mean the same bin on every chart. They aren't the real lid colours, because a red lid next to a green one is the pair colour-blind readers mix up most.

Why

Councils publish their waste figures one at a time, in spreadsheets. Side by side they show a gap in service that residents have no easy way to see.

Who

Anyone in Victoria who puts a bin out. No statistics background needed; terms are explained where they first come up.

What

Five government datasets: 23 years of kerbside tonnages and five years of costs for all 79 councils, council boundaries, 664 waste facilities and the 2021 census socio-economic index.

How

Maps where the pattern is about place, one dot per council where it's about spread, so you can count the councils behind each point.

01What we throw out

Almost half of it is still general garbage

Victoria's councils picked up 2.35 million tonnes from kerbside bins in 2023–24. First, what was in it, and how much each household put out.

What was in Victoria's kerbside bins, 2023–24

Nearly half of everything we put out still goes in the garbage bin, which means it gets buried. Food and garden waste is the next biggest share, and all of that could be composted.

Recycling Victoria, VLGAS release 2025.03
47%

of what goes out on the kerb still goes in the red-lid bin and straight to landfill.

Food and garden waste is 31%, a bit more than the yellow recycling bin. Glass is under 1%, mostly because only 16 councils have a glass bin yet.

How much waste each household puts out a week, 2023–24

The typical household puts out about 17 kg a week. In inner Melbourne it's less than half that, mostly because apartments don't have gardens.

Recycling Victoria, VLGAS release 2025.03
7 kg

a week per household in Yarra and Melbourne City, where a lot of people live in apartments.

It's around 23 kg in Greater Shepparton, Nillumbik and Indigo. Bigger gardens mean more green waste.

So there's a lot of waste, and where you live changes how much you make. The next question is how much of it each council manages to keep out of landfill.

02How much stays out of landfill

The typical council keeps about half out of landfill

The middle council in Victoria keeps 50% of its kerbside waste out of landfill, so half gets recycled or composted and half gets buried. Some councils do a lot better than that and some a lot worse.

Share of each council's waste kept out of landfill, 2024–25

Alpine Shire keeps 74% out, so only about a quarter of its waste gets buried. West Wimmera is the other way round: it keeps 16% out, so more than four of every five kilos end up in landfill.

Local Government Victoria, LGPRF indicator WC5, 2024–25. Each council's own reported figure.

The same councils, each sized by how much waste it collects

Sizing councils by their waste changes the picture. The western shires with the lowest rates are tiny, while big outer suburbs like Wyndham, at 34%, collect a lot of waste and bury most of it.

Recycling Victoria, VLGAS 2023–24 tonnes, and Local Government Victoria, LGPRF 2024–25 diversion. Circle positions worked out for this page.

The ten councils with the lowest rates only collect about 4% of the state's waste between them. Wyndham sends about as much to landfill as all ten put together, simply because it's so much bigger. So if Victoria wants to lift its overall rate, Melbourne's big suburbs matter more than the small shires. That brings up the obvious question: are city councils just better or worse than country ones?

03The bins

The green bin makes the biggest difference

Councils offer anywhere from two bins to four. The one that matters most is the green bin, because food and garden waste is almost a third of what we throw out. Without a green bin, all of it goes in the garbage and gets buried.

Share kept out of landfill, grouped by the bins a council offers, 2024–25

Councils with a green bin keep about 54% out of landfill, and councils without one keep about 31%. That gap is nearly a quarter of everything a household throws out. Adding a glass bin as well barely changes anything.

Local Government Victoria, LGPRF indicator WC5, and Recycling Victoria, VLGAS release 2025.03

Thirteen councils still don't have a green bin. Three of them, Hindmarsh, Yarra and Yarriambiack, have a glass bin instead, and Yarriambiack and Hindmarsh are second- and third-last in the state. So why haven't these 13 councils added a green bin?

04Why some councils don't have one

For the western shires, the nearest compost site is a long way off

Food and garden waste has to be trucked to a site that can process it. If the nearest one is far away, running a green bin costs a council more.

Where green waste can be processed, and how far the 13 councils without a green bin would have to send it

The processing sites are mostly around Melbourne and the regional cities. The western shires would have to truck their green waste 60 to 100 km, which is a big extra cost for a small council.

Recycling Victoria, VLGAS release 2025.03, and waste and resource recovery infrastructure map data, March 2025. "Organics" in the source covers all organics processing, not just composting.

Distance explains a lot of it, but not all of it. Ten of the 13 are about 30 km or more from a site, which is more than twice as far as the typical council that does have a green bin. Yarra, Murrindindi and Mansfield are within about 25 km and still don't have one. (Yarra's nearest site is a grease-trap service, so its real trip is probably longer.) For the rest, all that extra trucking costs money, which is where the next section picks up.

05What it costs

Councils that pay more per bin tend to keep less out of landfill

Councils report what it costs them to collect each garbage bin over a year. The typical council pays $138, and a recycling bin costs about half that.

Share kept out of landfill, by what a garbage bin costs the council, 2024–25

Councils that pay less per garbage bin tend to keep more out of landfill. The middle council in the cheapest group keeps 66% out; in the dearest group it's 39%.

Local Government Victoria, LGPRF indicators WC3 and WC5, 2024–25. Cost is each council's reported direct cost per kerbside garbage bin, per year.
6 of 13

councils without a green bin are in the dearest group, and only three are in the cheapest. You can see them as the orange and pink dots low on the right.

Melbourne City is the odd one out at $476 a bin, because of how much collecting it does in the city centre.

This doesn't mean cheap bins cause good results. Small remote councils have high costs and long trips to a compost site at the same time, so the two go together. It does make you wonder whether money is behind all of this, which is what the next section checks.

06Does money matter?

Money helps a little, but it isn't why bins are missing

The ABS gives every council a socio-economic score from the census. A score of 1000 is the national average, and higher means better off.

How well-off each council's residents are, and how much it keeps out of landfill

The darkest councils are both well-off and keep the most out of landfill. Half of them are Melbourne suburbs; the rest are places like Surf Coast, Macedon Ranges and Alpine.

ABS SEIFA 2021, Index of Relative Socio-economic Disadvantage, and Local Government Victoria, LGPRF 2024–25.

The link isn't clean. Melbourne councils score highest on the census but are only middling on diversion, and regional cities are the other way round. So some of what you see here is just the type of council.

How well-off councils are, with and without a green bin

If poorer councils were the ones going without a green bin, the left side would bunch up at the bottom. It doesn't. Those councils sit in the middle, with a typical score of 991 against an average of 1000.

ABS SEIFA 2021 and Recycling Victoria, VLGAS release 2025.03
991

is the middle score of the 13 councils without a green bin. For councils with one it's 1003.

None of the 13 are in the two poorest bands, and none are in the two richest.

So money is part of how well councils do, but it isn't why some of them are missing a green bin. Being small and far from a compost site fits those councils much better.

07What's changed

Garbage has stayed flat for twenty years while the other bins grew

Last, a look back at how things have changed since the early 2000s, and how the green bin spread.

Tonnes collected from each kind of bin across Victoria, 2001–02 to 2023–24

The garbage bin hasn't grown in twenty years, even with about two million more people. The growth went into recycling and green bins, which is why the state's diversion rate rose from 30% to 53%.

Recycling Victoria, VLGAS release 2025.03. Reporting before 2015 is patchier, so treat the early years as a rough guide.

What kind of green bin each council offers, 2015–16 to 2023–24

Most councils already had a green bin for garden waste. The big change was letting people put food scraps in it too, which went from 3 councils to 55.

Recycling Victoria, VLGAS release 2025.03. It starts in 2015–16 because earlier food-scrap counts aren't reliable.
3 → 55

councils took food scraps in the green bin in 2015–16. By 2023–24, 55 did.

Only 14 councils got a green bin for the first time over those years, taking the total from 52 to 66.

The state's plan is for every council to offer all four bins by 2030. Thirteen are still without the green one, and that's the bin that makes the biggest difference.

Conclusion

What it adds up to

City versus country doesn't matter much. Both keep about half their waste out of landfill, and pay about the same for it.

The bins do. Councils with a green bin keep about 23 points more out of landfill than councils without one, and a glass bin on top doesn't add much.

The 13 councils without a green bin are mostly small and a long way from a compost site, and they tend to pay more per bin. They aren't especially poor.

If Victoria wants to lift its overall number, the big outer suburbs are where most of the waste is. For the 13 councils still without one, the green bin is the obvious next step.

Notes on the data

  • Diversion is each council's own reported figure under the performance reporting framework. Where I needed years before 2020–21, I computed it from tonnages instead; the two agree closely (r = 0.95).
  • "No green bin" means the 13 councils with no kerbside organics collection. Ten of them have only two bins; the other three run a glass bin.
  • Distances to processing sites are straight-line from each council's geographic centre, which understates the road trip.
  • Victoria's container deposit scheme began in November 2023, partway through the latest year of data, so I haven't treated it as a measured effect.
  • Bin and service colours are based on the Okabe-Ito set and checked under deuteranopia, protanopia and tritanopia. Amounts use ColorBrewer single-hue scales, darker meaning more.
