Kerbside waste in Victoria's 79 councils

Where Victoria's rubbish goes

Each week, the average Victorian household puts out about 16 kg of rubbish, recycling and garden waste. Just over half is kept out of landfill.

Some councils keep much more out of landfill than others. It might seem like a city-versus-country story, but the numbers point somewhere else. Here's a look at what each council collects, what happens to it, and what seems to make the biggest difference.

Before you start

Four bins and one number

One number comes up throughout this page: the diversion rate. It's the share of a council's kerbside waste that is recycled or composted instead of sent to landfill. A rate of 50% means that, for every 10 kg collected, about 5 kg is kept out of landfill. A higher rate means more material is put to use again.

Red lid: garbage

Everything that goes to landfill. Collected weekly almost everywhere.

1.11 Mt in 2023–24

Lime lid: food and garden

Garden clippings, plus food scraps in 55 councils (often called FOGO). Usually composted.

718 kt in 2023–24

Yellow lid: recycling

Paper, plastics and metals, sorted at a materials recovery facility.

503 kt in 2023–24

Purple lid: glass

Kept separate so it doesn't shatter through the yellow bin. 16 councils.

16 kt in 2023–24

These four colours mean the same bin on every chart. They aren't the real lid colours, because a red lid next to a green one is the pair colour-blind readers mix up most.

02How much stays out of landfill

The typical council keeps about half out of landfill

The middle council keeps about half of its kerbside waste out of landfill. There's a wide spread around that midpoint: some councils keep much more out, while others send most of it to landfill.

Share of each council's waste kept out of landfill, 2024–25

Alpine Shire keeps 74% out, so only about a quarter of its waste gets buried. West Wimmera is the other way round: it keeps 16% out, so more than four of every five kilos end up in landfill.

03The bins

The biggest difference is whether a council offers a green bin

Councils offer between two and four kerbside bins. The green bin takes food and garden waste, which makes up a large share of what households put out. Without that service, organics may end up in the garbage bin.

Number of councils at each share kept out of landfill, grouped by the bins they offer, 2024–25

Councils with a green bin keep a larger share out of landfill: about 54%, compared with 31% for those without one. The difference is substantial. A separate glass bin, on top of a green bin, isn't linked to a similar increase.

Local Government Victoria, LGPRF indicator WC5, and Recycling Victoria, VLGAS release 2025.03

Thirteen councils still don't offer a green bin. Three of them, Hindmarsh, Yarra and Yarriambiack offer a separate glass bin instead. Hindmarsh and Yarriambiack also have two of the lowest diversion rates in the state.

What makes a green-bin service harder to provide in these places?

04Why some councils don't have one

For the western shires, the nearest compost site is a long way off

Food and garden waste needs somewhere to go. When a processing site is far away, transporting it can add to the cost of running a green-bin service.

Where green waste can be processed, and how far the 13 councils without a green bin would have to send it

Most processing sites are near Melbourne and regional cities. Several western shires are 60 to 100 km from the nearest mapped site, so a green-bin service could mean longer transport trips.

Recycling Victoria, VLGAS release 2025.03, and waste and resource recovery infrastructure map data, March 2025. "Organics" in the source covers all organics processing, not just composting.

Distance explains a lot of it, but not all of it. Ten of the 13 are about 30 km or more from a site, which is more than twice as far as the typical council that does have a green bin. Yarra, Murrindindi and Mansfield are within about 25 km and still don't have one. (Yarra's nearest site is a grease-trap service, so its real trip is probably longer.) For the rest, all that extra trucking costs money, which is where the next section picks up.

05What it costs

Higher collection costs often go with lower diversion

Councils report what it costs them to collect each garbage bin over a year. The typical council pays $138, and a recycling bin costs about half that.

Share kept out of landfill, by what a garbage bin costs the council, 2024–25

In this comparison, councils with lower costs per garbage bin tend to have higher diversion rates. The middle council in the lowest-cost group keeps 66% out of landfill; in the highest-cost group, it's 39%.

This pattern doesn't show that lower costs lead to better results. Smaller, more remote councils may face both higher collection costs and longer trips to processing sites.

What about the communities those councils serve, does disadvantage line up with lower diversion?

06Does money matter?

Money helps a little, but it isn't why bins are missing

The ABS gives every council a socio-economic score from the census. A score of 1000 is the national average, and higher means better off.

How well-off each council's residents are, and how much it keeps out of landfill

Some councils with higher socio-economic scores also have high diversion rates. But the pattern isn't consistent: Melbourne councils tend to score highly on the index while sitting closer to the middle on diversion.

ABS SEIFA 2021, Index of Relative Socio-economic Disadvantage, and Local Government Victoria, LGPRF 2024–25.

The link isn't clean. Melbourne councils score highest on the census but are only middling on diversion, and regional cities are the other way round. So some of what you see here is just the type of council.

How well-off councils are, with and without a green bin

Councils without a green bin aren't clustered at the low end of the socio-economic scale. Their middle score is 991, close to the national reference point of 1000.

991

is the middle score of the 13 councils without a green bin. For councils with one it's 1003.

None of the 13 are in the two poorest bands, and none are in the two richest.

The socio-economic scores don't explain why these councils lack a green bin. Distance to processing sites may be part of the picture, though these data can't tell us every reason a council makes that choice.

07What's changed

Garbage tonnage has barely changed; other streams have grown

Finally, a look at how kerbside waste collection has changed since the early 2000s, including the spread of green-bin services.

Tonnes collected from each kind of bin across Victoria, 2001–02 to 2023–24

Garbage tonnage is around where it was two decades ago, while more material is collected in recycling and organics bins. Over the period shown, the state's diversion rate rose from 30% to 53%.

Recycling Victoria, VLGAS release 2025.03. Reporting before 2015 is patchier, so treat the early years as a rough guide.

What kind of green bin each council offers, 2015–16 to 2023–24

Many councils already collected garden waste. The bigger shift was adding food scraps to the green bin: the number of councils doing so grew from 3 to 55.

Recycling Victoria, VLGAS release 2025.03. It starts in 2015–16 because earlier food-scrap counts aren't reliable.

councils took food scraps in the green bin in 2015–16. By 2023–24, 55 did.

Only 14 councils got a green bin for the first time over those years, taking the total from 52 to 66.

The state's plan is for every council to offer all four bins by 2030. Thirteen are still without the green one, and that's the bin that makes the biggest difference.

Conclusion

What it adds up to

Metropolitan and regional councils have similar median diversion rates and costs per garbage bin.

The clearest difference in these data is the green bin: councils that offer one have a median diversion rate about 23 percentage points higher. A separate glass bin doesn't show the same difference.

Many of the 13 councils without a green bin are small and far from a mapped processing site. They also tend to have higher costs per bin, though these comparisons don't show that distance or cost alone explains their services.

Large outer-suburban councils account for a substantial share of the state's waste, so changes there affect the statewide total. For councils without a green bin, the data raises a practical question: what would it take to make organics collection workable?

Notes on the data

  • Diversion is each council's own reported figure under the performance reporting framework. Where I needed years before 2020–21, I computed it from tonnages instead; the two agree closely (r = 0.95).
  • "No green bin" means the 13 councils with no kerbside organics collection. Ten of them have only two bins; the other three run a glass bin.
  • Distances to processing sites are straight-line from each council's geographic centre, which understates the road trip.
  • Victoria's container deposit scheme began in November 2023, partway through the latest year of data, so I haven't treated it as a measured effect.
  • Bin and service colours are based on the Okabe-Ito set and checked under deuteranopia, protanopia and tritanopia. Amounts use ColorBrewer single-hue scales, darker meaning more.
